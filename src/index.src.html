    Words to know

    Scam talk can sound technical. Here is what the common words mean.

    If it already happened

    Scams catch smart, careful people. What matters now is acting quickly. Pick what fits and follow the steps.

    Where to report

    • reportfraud.ftc.gov: report any scam to the Federal Trade Commission.
    • ic3.gov: report internet crime to the FBI.
    • identitytheft.gov: a personal recovery plan if your identity was stolen.
    • consumer.georgia.gov: Georgia Attorney General’s Consumer Protection Division, (800) 869-1123 in Georgia.

    A plan for your family

    Scammers count on people acting alone and in a panic. A small plan you agree on ahead of time takes the panic out.

    1. Choose a family code word

    Pick a word or phrase only your family knows. If someone calls in an emergency asking for money, even in a familiar voice, ask for the code word. Voice copying software makes a voice alone unreliable.

    2. Hang up and call back

    Agree that nobody sends money on a call or message alone. Hang up, then call that person back on a number you already have.

    3. Talk without blame

    If someone tells you they were scammed, start with, “Thank you for telling me. Let’s fix this together.” Shame keeps people quiet, and quiet helps scammers.

    4. Set up protection together

    Turn on two-step login for email and bank accounts, install updates, and consider a free credit freeze. Do it side by side, so everyone understands it.

    Accuracy: test it yourself

    You shouldn’t have to take anyone’s word for how a scam checker performs. These built-in tests run in your browser, on your device, and show their mistakes.

    How the testing works

    The test messages were written for this project and were not used to teach the word model. Each test checks something different, and each one explains why it matters. A score of 25 or more earns a warning, and 55 or more is called a likely scam.

    What these tests do not cover

    • They use a fixed set of messages. Scammers change their wording, so new styles can slip through.
    • They check the text of a link, not the website behind it.
    • They measure this checker only. A good score is not a promise about any single message.

    How the checking works

    Two layers work together, both inside the page:

    • Rules. checks look for pressure, threats, payment requests, private-information requests, fake or lookalike links and senders, and disguised letters. Each finding comes with a plain-language reason.
    • A small word model. A naive Bayes classifier learns from short example messages when the page loads. It adds a gentle nudge to the score (20%), and the rules carry the rest.

    The only thing this page remembers is your text size and contrast choice, stored in your browser. Your messages are never stored.

    About Second Look

    Why it exists

    Scam texts and emails reach people every day, and older adults are often targeted. Most checkers ask you to upload your message. Second Look gives plain-language reasons and keeps the message on your device.

    Who made it

    Second Look was designed and built by a student developer in Georgia.

    How it is built

    Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with no outside libraries, no servers and no tracking. The checking logic is in one file with its own automated tests, and the word model is small enough to train in your browser in a blink.
